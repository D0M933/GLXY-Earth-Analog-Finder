Where on Earth is most like the Moon and Mars?

Before astronauts and robots go to another world, they practise somewhere on Earth that feels similar. Choose a possible landing or base site, and we'll show you the Earth places that come closest, what they have in common, and where the comparison breaks down.

Step 1

Select a Potential Area

These are real candidate landing and base sites that space agencies have studied. Pick a world, then click a pin on the map or a name in the list.

    Step 2

    Tuning

    Every match is a blend of 11 features. Each target already counts its own signature features three times as much. Move a slider to tell us what you care about. This step is optional: you can go straight to the results.

    Step 3

    The Closest Places on Earth

    All 806 land areas of Earth (5° × 5° cells) and 16 small named sites (1° × 1° boxes, marked “small site”) are scored on the same 11 features as your target and ranked together by similarity. Their box scores are corrected with a correction measured on 36 sample cells (see Sources). The map and list show up to the 35 most similar (fewer if a filter leaves fewer areas); the numbered dots are the top five. Click a dot or a name to see why. Every Earth score is computed from a named dataset, see Sources & how it was built.

      What Earth can't copy
      HONESTY CHECK

      What Earth can't copy

      No place on Earth is a perfect stand-in. A good analog is close on some things and far on others. These differences apply to every site.

      Gravity

      The Moon pulls with about one-sixth of Earth's gravity, and Mars with about 38%. Walking, digging and driving all behave differently there.

      Air

      The Moon has almost no atmosphere, and Mars's air is extremely thin, far below even the top of Mount Everest. Earth sites can't reproduce a vacuum or Mars's air pressure.

      Radiation

      Earth's magnetic field and thick air shield us from most space radiation. Nowhere on Earth's surface gives you the radiation that crews will face on the Moon or Mars.

      Dust & rock

      Earth's wind and water wear dust smooth. Moon dust is sharp and clingy, and Earth ash or sand can only imitate it.

      Life

      Earth sites are full of microbes. That is great for practising how to search for signs of life, but it means Earth can't show us what a truly sterile surface is like.

      Sources & how it was built

      Everything the scores are made from, how each one is calculated, how the site was put together, and what it cannot tell you.

      Please read: the scores on this site are a draft for exploring, not for engineering decisions. Every Earth score is computed from a named dataset, but seven of the eleven are proxies (counts of mapped volcanoes and craters, remoteness, and a sample of a rock map), so they are definite numbers, not ground truth. The Moon and Mars scores are the team’s own estimates, except the cold values: Mars cold was checked against the Mars Climate Database for every Mars target, Moon cold uses published LRO Diviner results, and one value (Lacus Mortis, 25) is still an unverified estimate.

      1Data sources

      Each card names the dataset, who publishes it, and the one thing we use it for. Colour tags show the kind of publisher.

      Earth: what the 822 areas are scored from

      NASANASA POWER

      MERRA-2 climatology, 2001 to 2025: yearly air temperature, rainfall and snow depth. Feeds the cold, dryness and ice scores, and the facts panel.

      NASAEarth Impact Database table (NASA JSC ARES)

      182 confirmed impact structures with latitude, longitude and diameter. Feeds impact geology. We read it on 15 Nov 2026 and corrected one longitude that looks like a typo (Ames, Oklahoma, printed as 98.2°E, used as 98.2°W). The table is undated (its address suggests May 2018), so recent finds are missing.

      NOAAETOPO1 global relief

      Heights at 9 points per area (rugged ground), the 40 km elevation line, average height and slope. Reached through OpenTopoData, which only serves NOAA’s ice-surface file. We checked 60 points against NOAA’s own elevation service: median difference 3 m, mean 9.7 m, largest 71 m (interpolation), which does not change any score.

      NOAANCEI Volcano Location Database

      A US government service that republishes the Smithsonian Global Volcanism Program list. The only volcano source: it feeds volcanic basalt, lava-tube terrain and the volcano fact.

      ScienceCOMNAP Antarctic Facilities

      Council of Managers of National Antarctic Programs, hosted by the Polar Geospatial Center. The 75 stations marked “Open”. Used only for the Step 2 filter; covers Antarctica and the sub-Antarctic only. Cite as “COMNAP 2017”; free for educational, non-commercial use.

      ScienceMacrostrat

      University of Wisconsin–Madison, National Science Foundation funded, compiled from national geological surveys including the USGS. Rock types at 9 points per area: feeds dust, salts and minerals, and highland rock.

      Open dataNatural Earth

      Public-domain, cartographer-made data: towns of 20,000 or more (remoteness), playas (salts and minerals) and region names.

      Why Macrostrat and Natural Earth are still here: neither is a government or space-agency source, but we could not reach a government alternative without a login, so we kept them and say so. Wikidata, used in versions 3.2 to 5.1, is no longer used anywhere. Earth data was gathered on 14 Nov 2026 (806 cells) and 15 Nov 2026 (16 small sites).

      NASA’s 13 candidate landing regions (Nobile Rim, Amundsen Rim and de Gerlache Rim were added from this list in v4).

      ESAOxia Planum and Mawrth Vallis

      ScienceMars Climate Database

      NASAMars map: Hubble projection

      NASAWorldview / GIBS · Earthdata Search

      The 16 small named sites

      Each small site shows its own reference in Step 3: NASA, ESA, USGS, the US National Park Service, a NASA-funded expedition or a scientific paper. Two come from museum or news pages and are marked as such. We picked them from pages that name them as analog or training sites; three we could not source (a Utah desert site, Teide and Pinacate) were left out.

      2How the Earth scores work

      Every score runs from 0 to 100. Four are read straight from a measured grid; seven are proxies that count things a dataset happens to list. The formulas are written in the header of tools/build-v32.js.

      Rugged groundSpread of 9 heights: 0 for flat ground, 100 for 800 m or more.NOAA ETOPO1grid

      From scores to a ranking

      • Matching. For each feature we measure how close the target’s and the area’s scores are, then blend them into one similarity from 0 to 100. A target’s signature features count three times as much, and your sliders change the weights too (js/match.js). Bands: Loose 0–33, Partial 34–66, Close 67–100. It is not a probability and not calibrated: a random pairing scores around 40 to 50.
      • Stability line. The page re-ranks 1,000 times with seeded random nudges (weights ×0.5 to 1.5, target scores ±5, Earth scores ±3 for cold, dryness and relief and ±5 for the rest, all kept between 0 and 100) and shows how often each top area stays first or in the top five. Each percentage has a sampling error of about ±3 points. The nudge sizes and the bands (Stable at 70% or more, Fairly stable at 40% or more) are our choices. The seed makes the result repeatable.
      • Like-for-like elevation. The Earth line is 40 km long with a point every 4 km; Moon and Mars lines run 1 to 330 km and are finer. The page puts both on a 4 km spacing and the same window (long lines are cut into 40 km windows and the median is used) before comparing height range and slope. Lines under 8 km are too short to compare.
      • The 16 small sites use the same formulas on a 1° box. The 9 sample points are about 37 km apart, the NASA POWER value is the roughly 50 km climate cell at the centre, and the height line has a point every 2 km (tools/build-v5.js). A measured correction (v5.1) adjusts the six features where it could be fitted.

      3How it was built

      From the question to the finished page, in the order we did it.

      1. The idea and the Moon and Mars lists are ours.

        Our team chose the question and the 14 original Moon and Mars targets, then added six from NASA and ESA lists in v4. Each target is described with 11 scores from 0 to 100 (a twelfth, crew facilities, was removed in v5 because every target scored 100 on it).

      2. Cut the Earth into candidate areas.

        A regular 5° grid (about 550 km a cell) was laid over the land outlines and cells whose centre falls on land were kept: 806 cells (tools/make-grid.js). Nothing is pre-selected. Earlier versions picked 35 cells because they matched the targets and then ranked them against the same targets, which was circular; that step is gone. Area names come from the region and coordinates, and a name is shown only if it is specific.

      3. Gather the Earth data.

        Climate, heights, volcanoes, craters, stations, rock types and towns were collected for every cell and for the 16 small sites from the sources above, through a browser because the build environment had no direct internet access. The raw numbers are kept in tools/ so every score can be traced. A correction for the small sites was measured on 36 random cells (v5.1).

      4. Compute the scores and write one data file.

        tools/build-data.js packed the Moon and Mars lists and base maps into data/data.js; tools/build-v32.js adds the Earth areas and their scores; later scripts (build-v5.js, build-v51.js, build-v52.js) add the small sites, the correction and the NASA and COMNAP data.

      5. Match and rank in your browser.

        The whole list is ranked live when you press “See the closest places”. The page shows the top 35 (fewer if the station filter leaves fewer).

      6. Draw the maps.

        All three maps use the same flat layout: x = (longitude + 180) × 2 and y = (90 − latitude) × 2 on a 720 × 360 grid, so pins and layers line up. The Moon and Mars maps are resized JPEG copies of the NASA images in assets/. The Earth map is land and sea outlines traced into polygons (in data/data.js) and drawn as SVG with grid lines every 30°; it is for locating things, not measuring coastlines. Pins, zoom and colours are our own code (js/map.js, js/app.js), with no map library. The layer menus colour pins by a feature or add full-map NASA layers; we left out layers that were too noisy or incomplete (THEMIS infrared and the Diviner maximum and minimum maps). The layer list is in tools/set-layers.js.

      7. Build the page.

        Plain HTML, CSS and JavaScript with no libraries. Everything is static and can be hosted anywhere. The Moon is themed black and grey, Mars red.

      8. Test it.

        A headless browser (Playwright) steps through all 20 targets checking for script errors, and runs the three steps at desktop, tablet and phone widths checking for sideways scrolling and clipped text. We review the screenshots.

      9. AI help.

        Claude, an AI assistant made by Anthropic, wrote the code from our design. The concept, the data choices and the review are ours.

      What it needed: the NASA, NOAA and other datasets named above, an internet connection for the live NASA layers, Node.js to prepare the data, Playwright to test, and Claude to write the code. README.md lists every file in tools/ and how to run them.

      5.1Measured correction for small sites

      The v5 guess that small boxes score more extremely was tested and not supported. 36 random cells (tools/sample_cells.json) were re-scored as 1° boxes (tools/sample_boxes_raw.json, gathered 15 Nov 2026) and a line, cell score = a + b × box score, was fitted per feature (tools/calibrate-v51.js). A fit is used only if r² is at least 0.25 and the slope is between 0 and 1.5 (tools/build-v51.js); the uncalibrated box scores stay on each small site’s card. The sample is small (36 boxes, 6 in Antarctica), so each fit has real uncertainty.

      5Known limits and next steps

      Earth areas

      • Most areas are whole 5° cells, about 550 km across, so islands, craters and lava tubes are mostly not captured. A cell is kept by its centre point, so some coastal cells are missing and some mostly-sea cells are included. Polar cells are smaller on the ground and neighbouring ones have almost the same climate.
      • The proxy scores depend on coverage: the crater table has only confirmed impact structures (182 worldwide, so most areas score 0), the station list covers only Antarctica, the caves score is just basaltic volcanoes, and the rock map is sampled at 9 points. Highland rock is near zero almost everywhere on Earth.
      • Antarctic and Greenland cells have no snow measurement, so they are treated as full ice cover. Permafrost is inferred from air temperature, not observed. ETOPO1 terrain is about 2 km resolution.
      • Satellite images show a whole 5° area, not a close-up, and are loaded live from NASA.

      The 16 small sites

      • They are not a fair draw: we chose them because they are known analog sites. They appear in the top 35 about 0.95 times per target, against 0.7 if drawn at random from all 822. Read their position as “a known analog that also scores well”.
      • Test (v5.1): cold, dryness and ice are identical in boxes and cells (r² = 1.00, because the NASA climate grid is about 50 km either way); height variation is about 2 points lower in boxes, remoteness 4 lower, salts and minerals 9 lower. Counts of volcanoes, craters and caves are lower in boxes (a box is about 1/25 of the ground), but almost no sampled box had any, so no correction could be fitted for volcanic basalt, impact geology, caves, dust or highland rock.
      • Their climate is the roughly 50 km NASA cell at the centre, which can differ a lot from a summit or valley floor (Mauna Kea is a clear example). Real analog areas can be small, so a match applies to one spot, not a whole region.

      Moon, Mars and layers

      • Moon and Mars scores other than cold are hand-assigned estimates. Moon cold and ice come from published Diviner results, not yet computed from the raw files. The Lacus Mortis cold value (25) could not be verified. Mars cold is saturated: every Mars target colder than −50 °C yearly mean scores 100.
      • The six v4 areas have no elevation line yet. Leibnitz Beta Plateau, also on NASA’s list, was left out because we could not find coordinates for it. Pit sites have lines under 8 km, too short for the like-for-like comparison.
      • Layers: the Mars thermal-inertia and brightness maps are lower detail on NASA Trek. Mars has no true global temperature map, so that layer is thermal inertia, not temperature. The Moon Diviner layer is night-time only, covers 60°S to 60°N and is streaky where coverage is thin. Earth temperature is one month (July 2025) and snow is January 2025, so they are examples, not yearly averages. Layers load from NASA and may change or go offline.

      Before this is shared further

      • The world coastlines were traced from a Vemaps map; we have not confirmed that its licence allows reuse. Replacing them with Natural Earth coastlines would settle it.
      • Next steps we would take: compute Moon temperatures directly from Diviner files, find a global list of research stations and lava tubes of equal quality, and add more small sites that can be sourced to NASA, ESA or USGS pages.
